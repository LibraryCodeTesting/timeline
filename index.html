Section 2

1923 - March 29

Monmouth County Library is established by the County Board of Freeholders following a successful public vote in favor of creating a county library system. The initiative also has strong support from the County Board of Agriculture, the Grange, and the County Agent.

The Monmouth County Library Commission is then formed and appoints Elinor E. Randall to be the first County Librarian. Randall’s job begins on May 1st.

The library is expected to be fully operational on September 1st.

1923 - June 11

The County Library Commission is now planning where to create station libraries.

The goal for the station libraries is to make books more easily available to county residents than a city library system could. They are a mix of public and private locations that will house circulating collections for registered borrowers to use.

The first station libraries open in Holmdel, Rumson, and at the Woman’s Club in Ocean Grove.

Every other month, the stations are visited by the Monmouth County Library’s book automobile, which delivers a rotating selection of books for circulation.

The book automobile has been converted from a refurbished truck and weighs more than one ton. It can carry up to 800 items, including books, phonograph records, and pictures.

In addition to the station libraries, residents will also be able to request for materials by mail through the Librarian’s Office, located at the Courthouse in Freehold.

1924 - December 4

Bertram Borden, who is Chairman of the Housing Committee for the Monmouth County Library, requests that the county review options for making additional space for the library's main headquarters.

After more than 1 year in service, Bertram says that the library has, “completely outgrown its quarters in the basement of the courthouse.”

1924 - December 4 CHECK

Monmouth County Library’s collection now includes 23,875 books, which are available to patrons through a network of 40 library stations.

A new partnership with local schools has also begun. Teachers and students can now request books and other materials to be delivered directly to their schools. This allows teachers and students to order reference materials to supplment their lessons, or simply order items to enjoy as recreational reading.

To begin this program, a survey was sent to every school in the county, which lead to responses from 41 schools. The library then visited 26 of the responding schools, out of which 11 were then selected for circulating children’s collections.

1925 - July 6

Monmouth County Library's book automobile has delivered 35,183 books to the library stations across 515 deliveries.

In a single month, 2,000 books were distributed to the public through the station network.

1926 - February 8

The headquarters for Monmouth County Library has expanded into a remodeled boiler room located behind the Courthouse in Freehold. There are now 36,557 books in the collection, which is a 53% increase over 14 months. 3,738 of the books have also been delivered to directly fulfill patron book requests.

Books can be borrowed for as long as patrons need to finish them. The time can also be extended if the books are used for studies, or if someone lives far from a library station.

There are 11,170 registered patrons, many of which report that they borrow items for their family members and neighbors as well as for themselves. County librarians also say that metal workers, plumbers, carpenters, and other manual laborers also frequently request for materials to help develop new skills and improve their techniques

Library operations are now supported by a 0.2 mill tax contributed by towns that voted to join the county library system. This funding provides $20,770 for general library functions, and an additional $1,280 is allocated by the state to support the book automobile’s deliveries across 50 schools.

116 stations now exist, and the book automobile visits each one every 2 months. The largest stations are located in Allentown, Atlantic Highlands, Brielle, Farmingdale, Keansburg, Ocean Grove, and Red Bank.

The library has also announced that its primary goal for 1927 is to increase public engagement and demand for the library stations.

1927 - January 24

Monmouth County Library continues to grow quickly.

The headquarters in Freehold is once again reporting overcrowded conditions as the library's services expand.

The books have made 41,268 circulations in the past year, which was an increase of 10,000 more book circulations compared to the year prior.

Each library station’s collection is now tailored to reflect the interests and needs of its surrounding community. These preferences are communicated to Monmouth County Library by the local individuals who oversee each station.

To keep up with the increased demand, the number of library staff has also grown. However, some locations. such as the Keansburg location, can no longer rely solely on volunteers to maintain their scheduled hours and have begun transitioning to paid staffing.

1927 - October 19

The book automobile is now distributing approximately 4,000 books across 70 different schools.

1928 - January 12

The county library report shows that 51,220 books circulated in 1927. This is another increase of 10,000 circulations.

37,243 of the books were borrowed through just one of the library stations alone.

1928 - April

Schools are now using 5,517 reference books provided by the Monmouth County Library. The book automobile also currently runs across 25 routes over a two-month period. Each route includes between two and six library stations.

There are now 135 library stations, each of which houses between 50 and 1,000 books:

  • 46 in schools that only serve children
  • 24 in homes – mostly in farmers’ homes
  • 18 in schools that circulate to adults as well as children
  • 12 in stores
  • 8 in borough halls
  • 5 in library buildings
  • 5 in grange halls
  • 3 in post offices
  • 2 in community centers
  • 2 in churches
  • 2 in high school libraries
  • 2 in a Young Men’s Christian Association building
  • 1 in a women’s clubhouse
  • 1 in a summer camp
  • 1 in a firehouse
  • 1 in a parish house
  • 1 in a chapel
  • 1 at a road station

The following marketing tactics are being used to acquaint people with the Library’s services:

  • A librarian has joined the Grange to have direct contact with farmers and learn their needs.
  • Lectures about the library’s services have been presented at meetings of the Grange, the Young Men’s Christian Association, Women’s Clubs, schools, and other local organizations.
  • Local newspapers publish articles about the library, and advertise new or specialized books to the public.
  • The book automobile was intentionally designed to be easily recognizable in the community.
  • Library exhibits tailored for farm families have been created, featuring books on agriculture, materials for women and children living on farms, a large map showing the locations of library stations, helpful charts, and more.
  • Ongoing partnerships are maintained with a wide range of groups, including the the County Health Office, Parent-Teacher Associations, the Boy Scouts of America, YMCA, the County Board of Agriculture, the County Social Service Organization, school boards, Women’s Clubs, Mission-Study Classes, the Grange, the Farm Bureau, churches, and schools.
  • And finally, the library reports that, “Effective use is made of the library camera.”

Librarians also give the following examples of patron needs:

  • A farmer had requested a trade book that wasn’t in the library's collection. Monmouth County Library attempted to obtain a copy of the book by reaching out to six other libraries, the State Library, and even the publisher directly, but without any success. Finally, the Secretary of the State Library Commission was able to locate a private copy from someone that they knew, and then arranged for it to be loaned to help the farmer.
  • A farm boy with limited access to formal schooling borrowed a book on stationary engineering. After studying it throughout the winter, he passed a certification exam and secured employment as a stationary engineer himself.
  • A sheep herder borrowed a book on animal diseases when his sheep became sick. With the book, he was able to save his herd, while other flocks perished.
  • When the local potato crop failed and diversification became necessary, Monmouth County Library was ready to help local farmers to research their options by offering books on truck-gardens, fruit farming, dairy production, and poultry management.
Section 3

1932 - November 16

The custodians of the Monmouth County Library system meet in Red Bank Library to form the Monmouth County Library Round Table. The President of Red Bank Public Library, Henry S. White, is voted to be the Board President. All meetings will occur in spring and fall, and the next meeting will be in May at the home of Mrs. B. D. Yard in Allentown.

During this initial meeting, all participants reported increased circulation figures. This rise was attributed to the economic hardships of the Great Depression, after many unemployed individuals turned to the library to make use of their free time.

The largest problem among the libraries now is the lack of new books due dwindling funds. The library’s funding, which usually comes from taxes, is now unavailable due to so many being unemployed and living in financial strain. Members of the Round Table were encouraged to raise public awareness of the library's financial challenges.

1933 - January 19

The Neptune Library Branch has gained 140 new registrations and doubled its books in circulation from the year before: 8,500 book went out in 1931, and 16,000 books circulated in 1932.

The library is located at 204 Ridge Avenue, and is open on Thursdays 2-5PM.

1933 - May 26

The Monmouth County Library Commission entertains the organization’s library custodians at the home of County Commissioner B. D. Yard.

50 people from various Monmouth County Library branches and library stations across 27 towns and boroughs attend. There, they meet with the other County Commissioners while enjoying a series of events and lectures.

1935 - January 2

The Neptune Branch now says that 6,707 books circulated in the past year, which was a significant drop. The branch says that this is because it could not get more new books, which has impacted patron demand.

1937 - May 4

A fire spreads through a 4-car garage and destroys a Monmouth County Library book truck that was loaded with 1,300 books intended for Brielle Public Library.

Officials say the fire resulted from spontaneous combustion of debris in a different compartment of the garage.

1937 - June 1

The library states that 3,122 items were loaned to school children in Wanamassa during the last school year. This was an incease of 1,123 items compared with the prior year, showing that the demand for library services within schools is still icnreasing.

1938 - February 1

The Freehold Borough Council announces that Monmouth County Library can use what used to be the home of a former Borough Clerk as its official Headquarters Branch.

The library is still in a detached building behind the county courthouse and has once again outgrown its physical location. Sheriff George H. Roberts will now take over detached building behind the courthouse for his own department.

1938 - February 4

The Headquarters Branch of the Monmouth County Library has officially relocated. All books have been transferred to their now location within a house on 69 West Main Street in Freehold. The new Headquarters Branch will open to the public the following day.

Section 4

1940 - April 18

The Monmouth County Library Commission’s annual report shows 1,049 new borrowers joined the system. 360,518 titles have also circulated across 67 library stations, 57 schools, and 49 trailer stops.

1941 - May 18

Monmouth County Library’s new book trailer celebrates its third anniversary.

The trailer’s services are described as being as reliable as the USPS, since it operates in all weather conditions except when snowfall makes travel impossible.

Between 1939 and 1940, the trailer’s circulation numbers doubled purely through word-of-mouth recommendations. It continues to serve remote communities, including Vanderburg, Holmeson, Oak Glen, and El, by traveling across 10 bi-weekly routes.

Inside the trailer, patrons will find a desk, chairs, racks, electric lighting, and a collection includes 800 titles that is regularly freshened up. In the summer, electric fans keep the space cool, while a small coal stove provides heat in the winter.

Items from the book trailer are fine-free since borrowers could easily miss when it visits again. The County Library also states that their mission is to encourage use of its facilities within reason, rather than discourage the borrowing of books through late fines.

The trailer remains the only mobile library of its kind in New Jersey, and is reportedly one of only a few in the entire United States.

Currently the route schedule is as follows:

Week 1

Monday – Reeveytown, Cold Indian Spring, Asbury Gardens, and Summerfield

Tuesday – Allenwood, High View Park, Como and Spring Lake Heights, New Bedford

Wednesday – Port-as-Peek

Thursday – Fleetwood, East Freehold, Vanderburg, Phalanx, and Scobeyville

Friday – Tennent, Manalapan, Gordon’s Corner, Morganville, Wickatunk

Week 2

Monday – Hazlet, North Centerville, West Keansburg, Conterville, and Holmdel

Tuesday – Ely, Holmeson, Cream Rdige, New Sharon, Perrineville, and Sweetman’s Lane

Wednesday – Huise’s Corner, Freewood Acres, Oak Glen, Maxim, and Nist Farms

Thursday – Oakhurst, Loch Arbucur, Wanamassa

Friday – Howell state police barracks, Fitkin hospital nurses’ home, East Farmingdale, and North Farmingdale

1943 - February 25

The Monmouth County Library Commission has announced that the book trailer will be placed in storage and its delivery service temporarily suspended due to wartime conditions. Instead, a station wagon will be used as a "book truck."

The change is being made because the station wagon can also easily be driven by other people, and it consumes less gasoline - which is now subject to wartime rationing. The change will become effective on March 1st.

1943 - March 5

Oceanport has announced the opening of a new library station at the local firehouse to replace the services lost with the suspension of the book trailer.

The station will be open every other Tuesday from 1:00 to 3:00 PM.

1945 - April 5

A total of 43,595 books have circulated from 152 locations across 13 townships and 24 boroughs. In addition, residents have also shared more than 5,000 specific book requests.

1946 - November 17

With wartime restrictions now gone, Monmouth County Library announces that its demands and services are increasing even more.

The county system now serves 58 branch libraries and 61 schools. Two book mobiles are also in use, one of which can now hold 1,500 titles.

1947 - June 1

The Monmouth County Library has launched a public contest, inviting residents to design a new sign for display at library stations.

Entries must be submitted on paper or cardboard measuring 12 x 18 inches. Each design should feature a simple, book-related symbol that can be easily recognized from a distance of 30 feet, and may not use more than two colors.

Submissions will be sent to the Asbury Park Press, and the winning design will be announced on June 22 with a $50 prize. In addition to being displayed across the county, the winning sign will be showcased at the national library convention in California.

1947 - June 29

A winner has been selected in the contest to design signs for the Monmouth County Library’s book stations: William E. Kemble.

Kemble’s winning design features a yellow symbol set against a green background and represents the idea of “knowledge on wheels.”

1949 - April 18

The Monmouth County Library book mobile is still the only one of its kind in the state, and is now celebrating its eleventh year of service. It is funded by the communities it serves and drives 300 miles each week, and librarians report that during visits, it is not uncommon for a single individual or household to accept the responsibility of borrowing books on behalf of an entire neighborhood. The book mobile is also always staffed with a librarian trained in Reader’s Advisory, as well as a children’s specialist who works with local schools.

The book mobile's collection are updated every 6 weeks, and patrons can have unlimited checkouts. Books are rarely returned late, and a daily fine of 1 cent accrues if items do return overdue.

1949 - December 2

Communities that have joined the Monmouth County Library system currently contribute a library tax of 3/10ths of a mill on the assessed value of their property. For a property assessed at $1,500, which was the average at the time, that amounted to 45 cents per year.

The Library Commission is now proposing to increase this rate to 4/10ths of a mill. Under the new rate, the same property owner would pay 60 cents annually.

There has been a 16% increase in the area’s population since 1940, and the additional funds would enable evening hours in rural areas, and would allow purchased a new and more reliable “bookmobile” for $6,800. The current bookmobile makes weekly deliveries to 64 municipalities and 60 schools, while the book trailer manages 45 regular stops - including the Marlboro state hospital, the county welfare house in Freehold, and Fitkin hospital.

Section 5

1950 - February 5

Neptune opens a new library branch in the basement of the West grove Church on Atkins Avenue. It is open on Tuesdays from 2 to 5 PM.

The library hopes to inspire a renewed love of reading in children, who are now more often seen watching television than holding a book

1950 - March 23

Residents of Allentown and neighboring communities gathered to see the Monmouth County Library’s new bookmobile upon its arrival in New Jersey.

Designed by a firm in Ohio, the bookmobile was built to bring library services to rural residents and features a modern, all-metal body weighing two tons. It can carry up to 3,000 books, including 1,000 stored in a special cab-over-engine compartment.

The vehicle is equipped with two skylights for natural daylight, fluorescent lighting for nighttime use, the latest in heating and ventilation systems, and dedicated desk space for librarians and clerks.

1950 - July 5

The Monmouth County Library’s new bookmobile is formally dedicated in front of the Asbury Park Press offices in Press Plaza, alongside the original book automobile from 1923.

The new bookmobile will operate five days a week, serving 59 book stations and 42 trailer stops. At this time, a book station refers to a location where a permanent collection is housed and regularly refreshed, while a trailer stop is a temporary location where patrons can borrow items directly from the bookmobile before it continues to another stop.

Beginning in September, the older book trailer will be used exclusively for educational services, delivering materials to 60 county schools.

1950 - August 9

The Monmouth County Library Commission has received a $50 gift from the Futterman Trust to expand its collection of sound recordings (also known as records). The library now holds 600 records, which may be borrowed for two weeks at a time.

The Futterman Trust was established in honor of Harry Futterman, who passed away in 1945, and was a certified accountant in New York and a veteran of World War I. Futterman often recalled that music was what he missed most during his time as a soldier.

At the start of World War II, he founded Armed Forces Master Records Inc., an organization dedicated to distributing classical music to military personnel at posts, on ships, and in stations through a network of 1,500 libraries. He personally donated office space and secretarial support to ensure the effort’s success. After his passing, the Futterman Trust was created to carry forward his mission of bringing music to those in service.

1953 - April 30

Librarian Julia Killian reports that borrowers on Friday evenings have tripled in the past 3 weeks after the library began to remain open until 9PM.

1955 - August 7

A four-part series published in the Asbury Park Press draws urgent attention to the strained conditions of libraries along the Jersey Shore, particularly in Monmouth and Ocean Counties.

Over the past three years, committees working with the State Bureau of Public and School Library Services have conducted studies and gathered testimony, ultimately revealing that only two library locations in the shore area have budgets large enough to adequately support their operations.

The Funding Gap

At this time, New Jersey State Library Association recommends allocating $2.50 per person, per year to meet current demand for library services, and emphasizes that $1.50 per person is the minimum needed to maintain basic library operations.

However, Ocean County Library receives just over $1.00 per person annually, and Monmouth County Library receives only 70 cents per person, which is less than one-third of the recommended amount. Of that 70 cents per person that Monmouth County Library has, less than half is used for staffing, and most of the funding is used to continue collection development and other services, such as maintaining the bookmobile.

Heavy Usage, Low Funding

Despite these budget shortfalls, Monmouth County Library circulates three books for every two residents, amounting to an average cost of just 50 cents per book borrowed. The bookmobile alone circulated 57,000 books in 1954, a number that does not account for the multiple readers of each book, particularly in schools where a single book may be read 15 times or more.

While donations help, the article series makes it clear: an increase in library tax funding is necessary to sustain and expand services.

Structural Challenges & Recommendations

A major inefficiency lies in the patchwork of library participation across the county. Not all towns have joined the county library system, and this has caused duplicated purchasing, increased reliance on bookmobile services, and higher public spending with less impact.

To address this, the State Library Association recommends the following actions:

• Construct central library buildings to consolidate collections and reduce bookmobile dependence

• Coordinate system-wide book orders to avoid duplicate purchasing.

• Establish a central cataloging department for all to share, rather than have multiple people and locations fund and invest time on duplicate cataloging.

• Collaboration among school, college, and public libraries to share materials and address collection gaps.

The report outlines several aspirational and achievable goals:

• Every resident should have access to a central library within 30 minutes of their home, with at least 25,000 well-selected, up-to-date books. Currently, only two locations in the entire county meet this standard.

• Collections should include not only books, but current magazines, films, sound recordings, reference materials, and a comfortable reading room as well.

• Every library and bookmobile stop should be staffed by a professional librarian and a children’s specialist, rather than rely on volunteers. Currently, only two locations employ professional librarians.

• Libraries should offer programs, activities, and specialized services for all age groups in addition to physical item collections.

• A coordinated, statewide plan should allow research libraries and state universities to share their collections with the public.

In addition to the population changes around the shore area, librarians are also reporting a sharp rise in demand for specific titles after they are mentioned on television, and the demand can be hard to keep up with on such short notice.

1956 - February 8

Librarian Julia Killian reports that the Monmouth County Library now serves a population of 168,000 residents. The bookmobile continues to play a vital role, delivering materials to 62 schools that collectively educate 21,000 students.

Killian also takes time to emphasize the impact that television is having on reading trends once more: rather than discouraging reading, television is now contributing to an increased demand for books, particularly when titles are mentioned or adapted on screen.

1956 - September 22

A local resident publishes a letter in the Asbury Park Press expressing concern about Monmouth County Library’s limited hours.

While the resident commended the library for recently extending its Friday hours to 9:00 PM, but noted that the library closes at 4:30 PM on other weekdays. They explain that this time is too early for many working professionals and commuters. They also point out that the library remains closed on Saturdays, which is often the only free day available for people to study, conduct research, or browse for recreational reading.

The letter further highlights concern over declining services in at least one rural area, even as the population in rural parts of the county continues to grow.

The writer advocates for expanded services and more practical, convenient library hours, especially in rural areas, emphasizing that such improvements would help build a stronger, more informed community.

1956 - September 30th

Staff shortages and delayed building improvements are beginning to take a toll on the Monmouth County Library system.

Newer staff are working diligently to maintain collections and equipment in aging facilities, and librarians are limited in what they can earn. Even after years of service, many cannot earn more than $2,500 per, which is making recruitment and retention increasingly challenging.

Budgets also remain extremely limited, leaving many locations unable to purchase more than a few new items, if any at all. This is especially affecting the acquisition of essential resources such as encyclopedias, updated business directories, technical and professional literature for continuing education, magazines, and even a broader selection of current fiction and children’s books.

1956 - November 1

Library Director Julia Killian reports a sharp rise in demand for library services, driven by an influx of new residents moving into housing developments in rural areas of Monmouth County.

To meet this growing need, the library has added 10 new bookmobile stations with permanent collections, bringing the total number of rural stations maintained by the bookmobile to 80.

The demand for library materials among schoolchildren has also grown so significantly that books are now being rationed. Kindergarten through 3rd grade classes may borrow 25 shared books per class. 4th grade classes and above are allowed 3 books for every 2 students.

Currently, 30,000 students are borrowing books from Monmouth County Library through their schools.

The American Library Association recommends that libraries provide five books per child, which would require $1.50 in library taxes per person. At present, Monmouth County Library receives only $1 per family.

1957 - January 20

Monmouth County Library now uses a multi-step process to recover overdue books, helping to preserve both its collection and its limited budget.

When an item becomes overdue:

1. A mailed notice is sent to the patron, including the replacement cost.

2. If the item is still not returned, the patron receives a follow-up phone call.

3. As a final step, a librarian visits the patron’s home, which results in the successful return of materials 75% of the time.

This approach is especially important because replacement fees can not only strain the budget, but the replacement costs can double for out-of-print books.

1957 - March 3

Librarian Rowland Mauch has launched a one-person campaign to generate interest in establishing a municipal library in Neptune Township.

Currently, the township’s library collection consists of 300 donated books housed in a corner of the welfare office, with additional books rotated in by the bookmobile periodically. The collection is open to the public only on Tuesdays from noon to 4 PM.

On February 7th, Mauch organized a “clean the attic” campaign to collect more donated books for the location’s permanent collection, which does not get rotated out among the other locations and stations. She believes the growing township needs a formal library building, and has ordered shelving to hold an additional 1,000 books. Her most pressing need currently is for reference books, which are too expensive to purchase within the current budget.

Mauch is also promoting the idea of creating memorial dedications within books, funded by donations to support fundraising efforts for a permanent library location. However, she emphasizes that residents must want a library and actively participate in its creation for it to become a reality.

The township government has expressed a cautious stance, stating they want to observe how the library develops in its current space before considering building a permanent facility. Meanwhile, the existing budget will be stretched to purchase the most essential materials.

1957 - May 5

Asbury Park Library, which operates independently of the Monmouth County Library system, reports that it is currently purchasing fewer books than during the Great Depression due to severe funding constraints.

Director Julia Killian of Monmouth County Library also notes that the cost of books has increased by 75% over the past 25 years, a trend that significantly impacts the county library system, which is the largest book purchaser in the region.

1957 - May 8

The Shrewsbury Borough Council has approved an emergency appropriation of up to $3,000 to establish a physical library branch.

Until now, the borough has been served solely by the bookmobile but has long needed a permanent location.

The new library will house 3,000 books available for circulation, with an additional $500 allocated to purchase new materials. It is planned to be open three days per week.

1957 - December 1

Monmouth County Library’s collection now totals 125,000 books and delivers materials to 65 elementary schools and 5 high schools.

All residents and property owners automatically qualify for a library card, while other adults can obtain temporary membership by paying a refundable deposit. Many summertime residents use this system. Patrons aged 21 or younger must have parental permission to join temporarily.

1957 - December 26

Neptune resident Catherine Scott wrote to the Asbury Park Press to express her gratitude for Librarian Rowland Mauch.

Thanks to Mauch’s dedication, Neptune is now celebrating its third year with a physical library location, which now houses a permanent collection of 500 books. Scott also appreciates Mauch’s commitment in regularly driving to Freehold to rotate additional books from the Monmouth County Library’s circulating collection.

Mauch’s efforts have helped build a loyal user base, with 200 regular patrons making 2,678 circulations at the Neptune library alone, which is about 13 books per patron.

1958 - June 18

An ad is placed in the Asbury Park Press for temporary summertime residents who are visiting the shore.

During the summer months, visitors staying temporarily in the area can join the library by paying a $1 subscription fee and a refundable $5 deposit. As long as all borrowed items are returned and no overdue fines remain, the $5 deposit is refunded when visitors leave to return home.

1958 - July 10

The Holmdel Township Committee has approved temporary use of the township hall as a library station, staffed by volunteers. Currently, the township is served by monthly visits from the bookmobile.

The Committee has agreed to consider establishing a permanent library location in the township hall during upcoming renovation plans. At present, the township hall is being temporarily used by a school, but a new school building is nearing completion, which will free up space for expanded library services.

1959 - March 22

The bookmobile is now serving 70 schools across Monmouth County, reflecting growing demand for library services in the region.

Library Director Julia Killian reports that population growth along the Shore is placing increased pressure on the system, particularly in areas that lack permanent library locations. She also notes that the Headquarters location is becoming too small to effectively support the library’s expanding operations and staffing needs.

Section 6

1961 – July 17

The Wall Library Branch officially opens with a starting collection of 1,300 books.

It is open Monday through Friday from 10:00 AM to noon, and again from 7:00 to 9:00 PM on Mondays, Tuesdays, and Thursdays.

The branch is staffed by volunteers, a librarian, and a part-time clerk.

1961 – April 10

The 100 member families of the New Shrewsbury Library Association have agreed to convert the Tinton Falls Methodist Church, built in 1815, into Shrewsbury Borough’s first public library.

Monmouth County Library will supply 2,000 books for a permanent collection, provide 2,500 newer books to be rotated periodically, and purchase $1,000 worth of reference books.

Two librarians will work alongside 10 local volunteers to establish the library. Its hours will be 3:30–6:00 PM, then 7:00–9:00 PM on weekdays, with Saturday hours closing in the afternoons.

A general building fund will be launched in the near future, but the Methodist Church will serve as the library’s first location until a new building can be constructed.

1964 – May 13

Renee Swartz of Middletown is now serving as President of the Friends of the Monmouth County Library Association, which was founded in 1961.

She is distributing brochures at railway stations, stores, and shopping centers to highlight the library’s inadequacies and raise public awareness.

1964 – August 31

Renee Swartz publishes four articles in the Asbury Park Press over consecutive days, highlighting the urgent need for a modern County Library.

Over the past ten years alone, Monmouth County has seen rapid development, a surge in student enrollment, and a population increase from 240,000 in 1954 to 370,000, which is a 54% increase in residents. Swartz also cites an MIT article stating that half of all current scientific fields have emerged within just the last decade.

She explains that workers now need to retrain for new skills an average of three times during their careers, yet the County Library has only minimal reference materials, no foreign or domestic periodicals, no newspaper files, and no specialized data resources.

Swartz argues that recent modernization has created an urgent need for a dedicated reference center that is stocked with up-to-date scientific and technological resources. She emphasizes that everyone should be able to use this center — whether they have a library card or not.

Although she acknowledges that maintaining a robust reference collection will be costly, she believes it would benefit the entire community, and hopes for cooperative solutions that do not draw funds away from school budgets.

Currently, the library tax brings in only 70 cents per capita based on property assessments, while the American Library Association recommends $4 per capita. Monmouth County’s library tax is now lower than that of many rural areas, including in rural parts of Georgia. Swartz notes that even tripling or quadrupling the current tax would cost less than the price of one carton of cigarettes per year.

She also reports that students are traveling to Newark Public Library to complete their studies, because Monmouth County Library lacks the collections needed for local research and assignments.

Swartz urges residents to advocate directly for a new library with the County Freeholders, who are the locally elected officials, and explains that the current Headquarters building is now overcrowded and outdated.

While the Freeholders have promised to help in the near future, Swartz warns that until a new Headquarters is built, the library will remain stuck in the “horse-and-buggy” era.

1964 - October 5

Monmouth County Library now has ten times as many borrowers as it did in 1954. Item circulations have also doubled in the same period, and the collection has grown by more than 50%.

Director Julia Killian emphasizes the urgent need for a new Headquarters and a budget that meets modern demands: “No institution, no organism, can exist for long on a starvation diet. Libraries are no different.” She explains the additional funding is needed to build new branch locations and to hire trained professional librarians.

Dr. James Whitehead, Director of the upcoming Monmouth Museum, also speaks about the importance of a modernized County Library system: “There are no big city libraries in the county. We need an informed citizenry, and a library helps.”

1964 - December 2

The Greater Freehold Area Chamber of Commerce is now urging the County Freeholders to build a new library in the Freehold area.

The Freeholders have granted permission to begin planning for a new library location, and recent studies show that future development and the population center are expected to expand westward toward Freehold.

However, the Friends of the Monmouth County Library are advocating for the new library to be built closer to the coast, where it could more easily serve the bulk of the county’s current population.

1965 - January 27

Library Commissioner Edward Fahey has announced plans for a new $600,000 library and hopes to secure $137,000 in federal aid to help fund the project.

He believes that once the new library demonstrates its popularity, the local government will be more willing to provide additional support for the County Library system.

1965 - February 14

The Asbury Park Press has now published its own position, supporting the plan to build the new library in Freehold.

1965 - February 21

The Friends of the Monmouth County Library have announced to County Freeholder Benjamin Danskin their intention to push for the new library to be built along the coast. Currently, only one of the five elected Freeholders supports locating the library in the eastern part of the county.

Renee Swartz argues that the majority of the county’s population lives near the coast — and that coastal residents pay four times more in library taxes than residents in the rural west. While the County Library was originally created to serve a mostly rural population, Swartz says the county’s needs have shifted and the library must now follow where development and growth have concentrated.

Today, 85% of the county’s population lives in 41 coastal municipalities, while the 12 municipalities west of the Garden State Parkway hold just 15% of residents.

The Friends note that coastal towns contribute $144,321 in library taxes, compared to just $30,661 paid by the western region. Projections also show that by the year 2000, the western part of the county will have an estimated 375,000 residents, while the coastal region will have 525,000 — 40% more.

1965 - March 1

The elected Freeholders and the Library Commission will meet at the American Hotel at 6:30 PM to decide on a location for the new $600,000 modern library.

The County Government and Director Killian still support building the library in the western part of the county, while one Freeholder and the Friends of the Monmouth County Library continue to advocate for constructing it east of the Garden State Parkway.

1965 - March 4

Email Katz, a member of the Board of Education, advocates for building the new library in Freehold. Katz expresses frustration with Freehold Township, Englishtown, and Manalapan Township for not engaging more actively in the debate. Katz states, “The site for the new library has become a political issue and I deplore this.”

1965 - March 21

The Friends of Monmouth County Library publish another article in the Asbury Park Press to promote building the library in the coastal area:

1965 - March 29

A group of businessmen and professionals have formed the Central Location for the Monmouth County Library Committee to counter the Friends of the Monmouth County Library. The committee states its main goal is to refute arguments made by the Friends, who are advocating for the new library to be built closer to the shore.

“Since no one expects the Friends of the Library Association to present facts unfavorable to their preferred location, our purpose is to present arguments in favor of retaining the library in Freehold,” the committee explains.

They note that 22 municipalities are closer to Freehold than to Eatontown, the current preferred site by the Friends of the Monmouth County Library. The committee also argues that Freehold is the true geographic center of the county and projects that by the year 2000, 44% of the county’s population will live closer to Freehold.

1965 - June 11

The elected County Freeholders have reached a decision regarding the new library plans. Two new libraries will be constructed - one in Freehold, and another near the coast.

1965 - July 10

The Monmouth County Planning Board is studying 20 potential sites for the new coastal library. Once the study is complete, the findings will be submitted to the Freeholders for a final decision.

The coastal library is planned to be built first, followed by a new library location in Freehold behind the Courthouse.

1965 - August 24

After much debate over the location of the new reference center, the foundation has also officially been poured for the new Ocean Branch Library.

The new library, costing $250,000 and constructed by Henry Vaccaro, will include public meeting rooms to meet community needs and will allow for an expansion of the nearby Township Hall.

1965 - October 20

It was announced that the new coastal library will be built in Shrewsbury for $750,000, on six acres of land generously donated to the county by Genola Inc. The site is part of the former Howard Strauss estate.

The Strauss estate spans 200 acres along the border of Eatontown and Shrewsbury, located in a residential zone.

This location was the second most-preferred choice for the new library after the Thomas Meachem property at the southwest corner of Route 35 and Sycamore Avenue in Shrewsbury— which was not available due to land constraints.

The selection was made based on zoning compliance, ease of access, travel time, and the character of the surrounding community.

1966 - May 15

The Ocean Library is dedicated at 1:30 PM

1966 - October 15

The Wall Library is outgrowing its current location and has issued a warning that expansion is urgently needed. A librarian spoke at the Red Oak Women’s Club, encouraging community members to join the newly formed Expansion Committee.

The committee aims to raise awareness and support for expanding library facilities to better serve the growing population.

1966 - December 13

Director Killian confirms that the Eastern Regional Library in Shrewsbury will serve as a dedicated reference and research center.

The reference collection will focus on electronics, nature, marine biology, and other key industries. Categories have been carefully curated to support local educational needs, emerging technologies, and the county’s economic development.

1967 - April 6

After seven years of studies, hearings, and debate, the groundbreaking ceremony for the Eastern Branch Library takes place, marking a significant milestone toward expanding access to modern library services for Monmouth County residents.

1967 - April 13

The library proudly announces the installation of a new copy machine—the first in the county capable of printing on legal-size paper—enhancing patrons’ ability to reproduce important documents and materials.

1967 - August 12

EB under construction.

1968 - June 4

Director Killian announces plans to establish a film collection at Monmouth County Library, aiming to attract patrons who do not typically visit the library. She believes that offering audiovisual materials will broaden the library’s appeal beyond traditional book readers.

Killian hopes to partner with Burlington, Ocean, and Mercer County libraries to create a cooperative film-sharing program. She intends to apply for a $15,000 grant from the New Jersey State Library and seeks contributions of $5,000 from each participating county.

TOnce established, the film collections will be shared and rotated among the four counties. Additionally, Monmouth County Library will make projectors available for clubs and organizations to borrow for film screenings. These groups will pay a usage fee, and a trained librarian will operate the projector during events.

Each film currently costs between $400 and $600. To help build the collection, the library is considering joining the Canadian Film Circuit, which offers access to free films featuring travel to Canada.

1968 - June 24

The Marlboro Library branch is now open in a renovated former garage adjacent to the town’s police headquarters. It houses a collection of 3,000 books, and operating costs are comparable to the Bookmobile service it replaces.

Librarian Florence Voorhees, specially hired and trained for this new location, is exploring the addition of children’s storytimes. The library is open Tuesdays, Thursdays, and Fridays from 3 to 6 PM; Wednesdays from 3 to 5 PM; and Saturdays from 10 AM to 1 PM. Hours may expand if demand increases.

“For the first time since the founding Quakers brought books to the Topanemus area here in 1685, the township has its own library. … Unlike the 1685 founders, whose library consisted of a Bible and only a few books, residents using the new library will find more than 3,000 volumes …”

1968 - September 10

Director Killian and Assistant Director John H. Livingston have announced the launch of three new programs aimed at supporting the construction of the new Eastern Branch Library. These initiatives include a film collection, a vocational education depository, and an anti-poverty program featuring study assistance and storytelling hours.

1968 - December 3

Director Julia Killian announced her retirement effective February 1st, after 22 years as Director of Monmouth County Library. She will retire at age 63 and be succeeded by Assistant Director John Livingstone.

Killian’s library career spans over 40 years. After graduating from the College of Saint Elizabeth in Convent Station in 1927, she began as a librarian in the Schenectady school system in New York. In 1937, she earned her Master’s in Library Science from Columbia University while also studying German at the University of Munich under a German government scholarship. Following her service as a Red Cross Director in India during 1944, she became Monmouth County Library’s Director in 1947.

At that time, the library had a staff of two and an annual budget of $25,000. Under her leadership, the staff has grown to 52, the budget to $282,581, and the collection to 188,580 volumes—up from 64,000 when she started.

“The new library is going to open, and by the end of the year we will have a federal depository and a state depository—the things I have been fighting for 12 years,” Killian said.

She retires with a salary of $12,000. John Livingstone will begin his tenure at $15,000. Livingstone started as a circulation librarian at Trenton Public Library, later served as Reference Librarian at the New Jersey State Library, and holds a Master’s degree from Drexel University.

A retirement dinner will be held at the Shadowbrook in Shrewsbury, followed by a celebration at the American Hotel with library staff, commissioners, and members of the Friends of the Monmouth County Library Association.

1969 - January 8

Director Livingston announces that paperback books will be added to the Monmouth County Library collection.

“There may be some who look down their noses at paperback books, but Monmouth County Library does not,” he says.

Livingston notes the growing popularity of paperbacks, especially among younger readers. Currently, 1,600 paperback books have been purchased and are in circulation, making up 10% of all books checked out.

1969 - April 8

James W. Mancuso of Long Branch has been selected as the architect for the new Monmouth County Headquarters Library. The new building will be constructed across from the county courthouse in Freehold.

James Bryan from Newark Public Library will serve as a consultant on the project. He previously contributed to the design of the Eastern Branch Library.

While the Eastern Branch functions primarily as a reference and research center, the new Headquarters Library will focus on circulation services. It will also house the Library Administration, an extension services department, as well as processing and storage facilities.

1969 - September 21

Monmouth County Librarian Theodore Brown collaborates with Director Haviland of Asbury Park Library to establish a library at Lincoln Village, which is the city’s first integrated housing complex.

Librarian Mary Jones works at the Village Library, although the facility is in poor condition.

After receiving a federal grant of $40,752, Monmouth County Library partnered with several local sites to distribute the funds, including the Village Library, West Side Community Center, Red Bank, the Monmouth Community Action Project’s (MCAP) Block Stop building, and a location in Long Branch.

The Village Library relies heavily on local volunteers. For example, one resident donated a broken record player, which another volunteer is repairing for children’s use.

The Village Library opened recently in June, and its entire circulation system is managed by Librarian Jones herself through a single card box.
