Section 2

1923 - March 29

Monmouth County Library is established by the County Board of Freeholders following a successful public vote in favor of creating a county library system. The initiative also has strong support from the County Board of Agriculture, the Grange, and the County Agent.

The Monmouth County Library Commission is then formed and appoints Elinor E. Randall to be the first County Librarian. Randall’s job begins on May 1st.

The library is expected to be fully operational on September 1st.

1923 - June 11

The County Library Commission is now planning where to create station libraries.

The goal for the station libraries is to make books more easily available to county residents than a city library system could. They are a mix of public and private locations that will house circulating collections for registered borrowers to use.

The first station libraries open in Holmdel, Rumson, and at the Woman’s Club in Ocean Grove.

Every other month, the stations are visited by the Monmouth County Library’s book automobile, which delivers a rotating selection of books for circulation.

The book automobile has been converted from a refurbished truck and weighs more than one ton. It can carry up to 800 items, including books, phonograph records, and pictures.

In addition to the station libraries, residents will also be able to request for materials by mail through the Librarian’s Office, located at the Courthouse in Freehold.

1924 - December 4

Bertram Borden, who is Chairman of the Housing Committee for the Monmouth County Library, requests that the county review options for making additional space for the library's main headquarters.

After more than 1 year in service, Bertram says that the library has, “completely outgrown its quarters in the basement of the courthouse.”

1924 - December 4 CHECK

Monmouth County Library’s collection now includes 23,875 books, which are available to patrons through a network of 40 library stations.

A new partnership with local schools has also begun. Teachers and students can now request books and other materials to be delivered directly to their schools. This allows teachers and students to order reference materials to supplment their lessons, or simply order items to enjoy as recreational reading.

To begin this program, a survey was sent to every school in the county, which lead to responses from 41 schools. The library then visited 26 of the responding schools, out of which 11 were then selected for circulating children’s collections.

1925 - July 6

Monmouth County Library's book automobile has delivered 35,183 books to the library stations across 515 deliveries.

In a single month, 2,000 books were distributed to the public through the station network.

1926 - February 8

The headquarters for Monmouth County Library has expanded into a remodeled boiler room located behind the Courthouse in Freehold. There are now 36,557 books in the collection, which is a 53% increase over 14 months. 3,738 of the books have also been delivered to directly fulfill patron book requests.

Books can be borrowed for as long as patrons need to finish them. The time can also be extended if the books are used for studies, or if someone lives far from a library station.

There are 11,170 registered patrons, many of which report that they borrow items for their family members and neighbors as well as for themselves. County librarians also say that metal workers, plumbers, carpenters, and other manual laborers also frequently request for materials to help develop new skills and improve their techniques

Library operations are now supported by a 0.2 mill tax contributed by towns that voted to join the county library system. This funding provides $20,770 for general library functions, and an additional $1,280 is allocated by the state to support the book automobile’s deliveries across 50 schools.

116 stations now exist, and the book automobile visits each one every 2 months. The largest stations are located in Allentown, Atlantic Highlands, Brielle, Farmingdale, Keansburg, Ocean Grove, and Red Bank.

The library has also announced that its primary goal for 1927 is to increase public engagement and demand for the library stations.

1927 - January 24

Monmouth County Library continues to grow quickly.

The headquarters in Freehold is once again reporting overcrowded conditions as the library's services expand.

The books have made 41,268 circulations in the past year, which was an increase of 10,000 more book circulations compared to the year prior.

Each library station’s collection is now tailored to reflect the interests and needs of its surrounding community. These preferences are communicated to Monmouth County Library by the local individuals who oversee each station.

To keep up with the increased demand, the number of library staff has also grown. However, some locations. such as the Keansburg location, can no longer rely solely on volunteers to maintain their scheduled hours and have begun transitioning to paid staffing.

1927 - October 19

The book automobile is now distributing approximately 4,000 books across 70 different schools.

1928 - January 12

The county library report shows that 51,220 books circulated in 1927. This is another increase of 10,000 circulations.

37,243 of the books were borrowed through just one of the library stations alone.

1928 - April

Schools are now using 5,517 reference books provided by the Monmouth County Library. The book automobile also currently runs across 25 routes over a two-month period. Each route includes between two and six library stations.

There are now 135 library stations, each of which houses between 50 and 1,000 books:

  • 46 in schools that only serve children
  • 24 in homes – mostly in farmers’ homes
  • 18 in schools that circulate to adults as well as children
  • 12 in stores
  • 8 in borough halls
  • 5 in library buildings
  • 5 in grange halls
  • 3 in post offices
  • 2 in community centers
  • 2 in churches
  • 2 in high school libraries
  • 2 in a Young Men’s Christian Association building
  • 1 in a women’s clubhouse
  • 1 in a summer camp
  • 1 in a firehouse
  • 1 in a parish house
  • 1 in a chapel
  • 1 at a road station

The following marketing tactics are being used to acquaint people with the Library’s services:

  • A librarian has joined the Grange to have direct contact with farmers and learn their needs.
  • Lectures about the library’s services have been presented at meetings of the Grange, the Young Men’s Christian Association, Women’s Clubs, schools, and other local organizations.
  • Local newspapers publish articles about the library, and advertise new or specialized books to the public.
  • The book automobile was intentionally designed to be easily recognizable in the community.
  • Library exhibits tailored for farm families have been created, featuring books on agriculture, materials for women and children living on farms, a large map showing the locations of library stations, helpful charts, and more.
  • Ongoing partnerships are maintained with a wide range of groups, including the the County Health Office, Parent-Teacher Associations, the Boy Scouts of America, YMCA, the County Board of Agriculture, the County Social Service Organization, school boards, Women’s Clubs, Mission-Study Classes, the Grange, the Farm Bureau, churches, and schools.
  • And finally, the library reports that, “Effective use is made of the library camera.”

Librarians also give the following examples of patron needs:

  • A farmer had requested a trade book that wasn’t in the library's collection. Monmouth County Library attempted to obtain a copy of the book by reaching out to six other libraries, the State Library, and even the publisher directly, but without any success. Finally, the Secretary of the State Library Commission was able to locate a private copy from someone that they knew, and then arranged for it to be loaned to help the farmer.
  • A farm boy with limited access to formal schooling borrowed a book on stationary engineering. After studying it throughout the winter, he passed a certification exam and secured employment as a stationary engineer himself.
  • A sheep herder borrowed a book on animal diseases when his sheep became sick. With the book, he was able to save his herd, while other flocks perished.
  • When the local potato crop failed and diversification became necessary, Monmouth County Library was ready to help local farmers to research their options by offering books on truck-gardens, fruit farming, dairy production, and poultry management.
Section 3

1932 - November 16

The custodians of the Monmouth County Library system meet in Red Bank Library to form the Monmouth County Library Round Table. The President of Red Bank Public Library, Henry S. White, is voted to be the Board President. All meetings will occur in spring and fall, and the next meeting will be in May at the home of Mrs. B. D. Yard in Allentown.

During this initial meeting, all participants reported increased circulation figures. This rise was attributed to the economic hardships of the Great Depression, after many unemployed individuals turned to the library to make use of their free time.

The largest problem among the libraries now is the lack of new books due dwindling funds. The library’s funding, which usually comes from taxes, is now unavailable due to so many being unemployed and living in financial strain. Members of the Round Table were encouraged to raise public awareness of the library's financial challenges.

1933 - January 19

The Neptune Library Branch has gained 140 new registrations and doubled its books in circulation from the year before: 8,500 book went out in 1931, and 16,000 books circulated in 1932.

The library is located at 204 Ridge Avenue, and is open on Thursdays 2-5PM.

1933 - May 26

The Monmouth County Library Commission entertains the organization’s library custodians at the home of County Commissioner B. D. Yard.

50 people from various Monmouth County Library branches and library stations across 27 towns and boroughs attend. There, they meet with the other County Commissioners while enjoying a series of events and lectures.

1935 - January 2

The Neptune Branch now says that 6,707 books circulated in the past year, which was a significant drop. The branch says that this is because it could not get more new books, which has impacted patron demand.

1937 - May 4

A fire spreads through a 4-car garage and destroys a Monmouth County Library book truck that was loaded with 1,300 books intended for Brielle Public Library.

Officials say the fire resulted from spontaneous combustion of debris in a different compartment of the garage.

1937 - June 1

The library states that 3,122 items were loaned to school children in Wanamassa during the last school year. This was an incease of 1,123 items compared with the prior year, showing that the demand for library services within schools is still icnreasing.

1938 - February 1

The Freehold Borough Council announces that Monmouth County Library can use what used to be the home of a former Borough Clerk as its official Headquarters Branch.

The library is still in a detached building behind the county courthouse and has once again outgrown its physical location. Sheriff George H. Roberts will now take over detached building behind the courthouse for his own department.

1938 - February 4

The Headquarters Branch of the Monmouth County Library has officially relocated. All books have been transferred to their now location within a house on 69 West Main Street in Freehold. The new Headquarters Branch will open to the public the following day.

Section 4

1940 - April 18

The Monmouth County Library Commission’s annual report shows 1,049 new borrowers joined the system, and 360,518 titles circulated among 67 library stations, 57 schools, and 49 trailer stops.

1941 - May 18

Monmouth County’s trailer library celebrates its 3rd birthday.

The trailer’s services are as reliable as the USPS, since it operates in all weather conditions except when so much snow falls that excavation is impossible.

Its circulation numbers doubled between 1939 and 1940 - entirely through word-of-mouth recommendations, and it still brings materials to out-of-reach communities – such as Vanderburg, Holmeson, Oak Glen, and El. The trailer travels along 10 routes on a bi-weekly schedule.

Inside, there is a desk, chairs, racks, and lights. Patrons can choose among 800 titles, and fans are installed in the summertime, then a small coal stove heats the trailer’s inside during the winter.

Items from the book trailer are fine-free when overdue since borrowers could easily miss when it visits again, and the aim of the county is to encourage use of its facilities within reason rather than discourage the borrowing of books.

It is still the only mobile trailer library in the state, and there are “probably very few in the United States”.

Currently the route schedule is as follows:

Week 1

Monday – Reeveytown, Cold Indian Spring, Asbury Gardens, and Summerfield

Tuesday – Allenwood, High View Park, Como and Spring Lake Heights, New Bedford

Wednesday – Port-as-Peek

Thursday – Fleetwood, East Freehold, Vanderburg, Phalanx, and Scobeyville

Friday – Tennent, Manalapan, Gordon’s Corner, Morganville, Wickatunk

Week 2

Monday – Hazlet, North Centerville, West Keansburg, Conterville, and Holmdel

Tuesday – Ely, Holmeson, Cream Rdige, New Sharon, Perrineville, and Sweetman’s Lane

Wednesday – Huise’s Corner, Freewood Acres, Oak Glen, Maxim, and Nist Farms

Thursday – Oakhurst, Loch Arbucur, Wanamassa

Friday – Howell state police barracks, Fitkin hospital nurses’ home, East Farmingdale, and North Farmingdale

1943 - February 25

The library commission announces that wartime conditions have forced the book trailer to be stored away and temporarily cease its delivery services.

Instead, the station wagon will be used as a “book truck” since it uses less gas – which is now being rationed. The station wagon can easily be driven by other people. The change will become effective on March 1st.

1943 - March 5

Oceanport announces that a library station will open at the firehouse to remedy losing its ability to get services from the book trailer. The station will be open every other Tuesday, 1-3PM

1945 - April 5

A total of 43,595 books are circulated from 152 locations across 13 townships and 24 borosoughs. More than 5,000 specific book requests were also made by residents.

1946 - November 17

With wartime restrictions gone, Monmouth County Library announces that its demands and services are increasing.

The county system now serves 58 branch libraries and 61 schools. Two book mobiles are in use, one of which can now hold 1,500 titles.

1947 - June 1

The county library creates a public contest, and asks people to submit their best ideas for a sign that will be displayed at library stations.

The submissions must be on paper of cardboard that is 12x18 inches. The sign must have a symbol that can be easily recognized from 30 feet away, may not use more than 2 colors, and the symbol should be simple and pertain to books. Entries will be submitted to the Asbury Park Press, the winner will be announced June 22 and receive a $50 prize. A copy of the winning sign will also be immediately flown at the national library convention meeting in California.

1947 - June 29

A winner is selected for the design of the signs for the county library’s book stations! William E. Kemble. Kemble’s submission is yellow with a green background, and symbolizes, “knowledge on wheels.”

1940 - April 18

The book trailer is still unique in the state, and is now celebrating its eleventh year of service.

Its functions are funded by the communities it serves, and it drives 300 miles each week. Librarians report than sometimes one person or home will accept responsibility for taking out books of whole neighborhoods during book trailer visits. It is staffed with a librarian trained in Reader’s Advisory and a children’s specialist to work with schools.

Book Mobile Highlights: Collection are updated every 6 weeks, patrons can have unlimited checkouts, and books are rarely late. If they are late, then there is a daily fine of 1 cent.

1940 - April 18

Areas that joined the Monmouth County Library system now pay 3/10ths of a mill on the assessed valuation of a property as a library tax. So, a property assessed at 1,500 (the average) would pay 45 cents.

The commission now seeks to raise this to 4/10ths of a mill, to expand and improve the library’s services. This would mean that the same property owner would now pay 60 cents per year.

There has been a 16% increase in the area’s population since 1940, and the additional funds would enable evening hours in rural areas, and a new and more reliable “bookmobile” can be purchased for $6,800. Currently, the bookmobile makes weekly deliveries to 64 municipalities, and 60 schools, as well as 45 trailer stops. It also regularly visits the Marlboro state hospital, the county welfare house in Freehold, and Fitkin hospital.
